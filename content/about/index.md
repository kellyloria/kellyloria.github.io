As a freshwater scientist, I’m interested in understanding how hydroclimatic controls on water availability (precipitation, streamflow, and evapotranspiration), along with human demand for water resources, shape ecological processes and ecosystem function in streams and lakes. My research combines field observations, long-term environmental monitoring, and quantitative analyses to understand how aquatic ecosystems respond to changes in climate, hydrology, and disturbance.

I have a special interest in mountain ecosystems and received my B.A. in Ecology and Evolutionary Biology from the University of Colorado Boulder (CU). I was fortunate to spend several years in the Green Lakes Valley, Indian Peaks Wilderness, and Rocky Mountain National Park surveying lakes and streams as the Limnology Program Manager for the Niwot Ridge Long Term Ecological Research Program. In this role, I investigated spatial and temporal trends in alpine zooplankton communities, lake water quality, and how climate change may alter physical processes such as lake ice cover, peak inflow timing, and thermal stratification, with implications for aquatic biogeochemistry and community ecology.

I received my Ph.D. in Ecology, Evolution, and Conservation Biology from the University of Nevada, Reno. My dissertation research with the Blaszczak Freshwater Ecosystem Ecology Lab investigated how hydroclimatic variability influences water quality and aquatic ecosystem energy fluxes, including gross primary productivity (GPP), ecosystem respiration (ER), and net ecosystem productivity (NEP), across streams and lakes. I examined how these processes vary between dry and wet years, across catchment characteristics, and under different streamflow regimes. I’m especially interested in how climate change may create phenological mismatches between physical hydrologic conditions and biological or biogeochemical responses in aquatic ecosystems.

I’m currently a postdoctoral researcher at the Desert Research Institute (DRI), working with Monica Arienzo, Brittany Kruger, and Mark Hausner to investigate how wildfire and changing hydrologic conditions affect water quality in western watersheds. Much of my current work focuses on stream networks impacted by the 2024 Davis Fire in the Washoe Valley region south of Reno, Nevada. Through field monitoring, water chemistry analyses, and hydrologic datasets, we are examining how burn severity, seasonal precipitation, snowmelt, and streamflow connectivity influence the mobilization and transport of nutrients, dissolved organic carbon, sediment, and trace metals following wildfire. I'm particularly interested in how these responses vary across watersheds and over time, including during storm events and seasonal transitions that can produce substantial changes in water quality.

In addition to field-based research, I’m working with collaborators at DRI to develop statistical and machine-learning approaches for predicting stream water temperature and dissolved oxygen across western watersheds affected by wildfire. This work integrates long-term monitoring records, climate and watershed characteristics, and disturbance histories to better understand when and where wildfire-related changes in stream conditions may emerge. More broadly, my research aims to connect changes in hydrology and water quality with aquatic ecosystem function, while developing tools and datasets that can help inform water resource management under increasingly variable climate conditions.
